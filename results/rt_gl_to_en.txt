Water is an inorganic compound with the chemical formula H2O. It is a transparent, tasteless, odorless and almost colorless chemical substance. It is the main constituent of the Earth's rivers, lakes and oceans. Water is also the fluid of all known living organisms, in which it acts as a solvent. Water, being a polar molecule, undergoes a strong intermolecular hydrogen bond that contributes greatly to its physical and chemical properties. It is vital to all known life forms, despite not providing food energy or being an organic micronutrient. Because of its presence in all organisms, its chemical stability, its worldwide abundance, and its strong polarity relative to its small molecular size, water is often known as the "universal solvent." Because the temperature and pressure of the Earth's surface are relatively close to the triple point of water, water exists on Earth as a solid, a liquid, and a gas. It forms precipitation in the form of rain and aerosols in the form of fog. Clouds consist of suspended droplets of water and ice, their solid state. When finely divided, crystalline ice can precipitate as snow. The gaseous state of water is vapor or water vapor. Water covers about 71% of the earth's surface, with the seas and oceans making up most of the volume of water (96.5%). Small portions of water occur as groundwater (1.7%), in the glaciers and ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland (1.7%), and in the air as water vapor, clouds (consisting of ice and liquid water suspended in air) and precipitation (0.001%). Water moves continuously through the water cycle of evaporation, transpiration (evapotranspiration), condensation, precipitation and runoff, usually reaching the sea. Water plays an important role in the global economy. Approximately 70% of the fresh water used by humans is used for agriculture. Fishing in freshwater and saltwater bodies has been, and continues to be, an important source of food for many parts of the world, providing 6.5% of global protein. Much of the long-distance trade in goods (such as oil, natural gas, and manufactured goods) is carried by ship across seas, rivers, lakes, and canals. Large quantities of water, ice and steam are used for cooling and heating in industry and homes. Water is an excellent solvent for a wide variety of substances, both mineral and organic; as such, it is widely used in industrial processes and in cooking and washing. Water, ice and snow are also fundamental to many sports and other forms of entertainment, such as swimming, recreational boating, boat racing, surfing, sport fishing, diving, ice skating, snowboarding and skiing. etymology
The word water comes from Old English wæter, Proto-Germanic *watar (source also Old Saxon watar, Old Frisian wetir, Dutch water, Old High German wazzar, German Wasser, vatn, Gothic 𐍅𐌰𐍄𐍉 (wato)), Proto-Indo-European *wod-or suffixed form of water *; Also related, through the Indo-European root, to the Greek ύδωρ (ýdor; from the ancient Greek ὕδωρ (hýdōr), whence the English 'hydro-'), Russian вода́ (vodá), Irish uisce and Albanian vạj. history
On Earth
properties
Water (H2O) is a polar inorganic compound. It is a tasteless, odorless, almost colorless liquid with a blue tinge at room temperature. The simplest hydrogen chalcogenide, it is by far the most studied chemical compound and is sometimes described as the "universal solvent" because of its ability to dissolve more substances than any other liquid, although it is poor at dissolving non-polar substances. This allows it to be the "solvent of life": in fact, water found in nature almost always includes various dissolved substances, and special steps are required to obtain chemically pure water. Water is the only common substance that exists as a solid, liquid, and gas under normal Earth conditions. states
Along with oxidane, water is one of the two official names of the chemical compound H2O; it is also the liquid phase of H2O. The other two common states of matter of water are the solid phase, which is ice, and the gaseous phase, water vapor or steam. Adding or removing heat can cause phase transitions: freezing (water to ice), melting (ice to water), vaporization (water to steam), condensation (steam to water), sublimation (ice to steam), and deposition (steam to ice). density
Water is one of the few common substances that expands and becomes less dense as it cools within certain temperature ranges; it is the only natural substance known to do so while in liquid form. It is also unusual in that it becomes significantly less dense as it freezes, although it is not unique in that respect. At a pressure of 1 atm, it reaches its maximum density of 999.972 kg/m3 (62.4262 lb/cu ft) at 3.98 °C (39.16 °F). Below that temperature, but above the freezing point of 0 °C (32 °F), water expands (less and less dense) until it reaches the freezing point, at which its density in the liquid phase is 999.8 kg/m3 (62.4155 lb/cu ft). When water cools to 3.98 °C (39.16 °F), the volume of water decreases. As it freezes and turns into ice, water expands by 9%, reaching a density of 917 kg/m3 (57.25 lb/cu ft). This expansion can exert enormous pressure, breaking pipes and cracking rocks. As a solid, it shows the usual behavior of contracting and becoming denser as it cools. These unusual thermal properties have important consequences for life on Earth. In a lake or ocean, water at 4 °C (39 °F) sinks to the bottom and ice forms on the surface, floating on top of the liquid water. This ice insulates the water below, preventing it from freezing. Without this protection, most of the aquatic organisms that reside in the lakes would perish during the winter. In addition, this anomalous behavior is an important part of the thermohaline circulation that distributes heat throughout the planet's oceans. magnetism
Water is a diamagnetic material. Although the interaction is weak, with superconducting magnets you can achieve a remarkable interaction. Phase transitions

However, even below the boiling point, water can turn to vapor at its surface by evaporation (vaporization throughout the liquid is known as boiling). Sublimation and deposition also occur on surfaces. For example, frost is deposited on cold surfaces while snowflakes form by deposition on an aerosol particle or ice nucleus. In the freeze-drying process, a food is frozen and then stored under low pressure so that the ice on its surface sublimes. Melting and boiling points are pressure dependent. Water can remain in a liquid state at high temperatures in the deep ocean or underground. For example, temperatures exceed 205 °C (401 °F) at Old Faithful, a geyser in Yellowstone National Park. In hydrothermal vents, the temperature can exceed 400 °C (752 °F). At sea level, the boiling point of water is 100 °C (212 °F). As atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude, the boiling point decreases by 1 °C every 274 meters. Cooking at high altitude takes longer than cooking at sea level. For example, at 1,524 meters (5,000 feet), the cooking time must be increased by a quarter to achieve the desired result. Conversely, a pressure cooker can be used to decrease cooking times by increasing the boiling temperature. In a vacuum, water boils at room temperature. Triple and critical points
On a pressure/temperature phase diagram (see figure), there are curves that separate solid from vapor, vapor from liquid, and liquid from solid. These meet at a single point called the triple point, where all three phases can coexist. The triple point is at a temperature of 273.16 K (0.01 °C; 32.02 °F) and a pressure of 611.657 pascals (0.00604 atm; 0.0887 psi); is the lowest pressure at which liquid water can exist. Until 2019, the triple point was used to define the Kelvin temperature scale. The water/vapor phase curve ends at 647.096 K (373.946 °C; 705.103 °F) and 22.064 megapascals (3,200.1 psi; 217.75 atm). This is known as the critical point. At higher temperatures and pressures the liquid and vapor phases form a continuous phase called a supercritical fluid. It can be gradually compressed or expanded between gas and liquid densities; its properties (which are quite different from those of ambient water) are density sensitive. For example, for suitable pressures and temperatures it can mix freely with non-polar compounds, including most organic compounds. This makes it useful in a variety of applications, including high-temperature electrochemistry and as an environmentally benign solvent or catalyst in chemical reactions involving organic compounds. In the Earth's mantle, it acts as a solvent during the formation, dissolution and deposition of minerals. Phases of ice and water
The normal form of ice on Earth's surface is ice Ih, a phase that forms crystals with hexagonal symmetry. Another with cubic crystal symmetry, ice Ic, can occur in the upper atmosphere. As the pressure increases, the ice forms other crystalline structures. As of 2024, twenty have been experimentally confirmed and several more are theoretically predicted. The eighteenth form of ice, ice XVIII, a superionic, face-centered phase of ice, was discovered when a drop of water was subjected to a shock wave that raised the water's pressure to millions of atmospheres and its temperature to thousands of degrees, resulting in a structure of rigid oxygen atoms in which hydrogen atoms flowed freely. When sandwiched between layers of graphene, the ice forms a square lattice. The details of the chemical nature of liquid water are not well understood; some theories suggest that its unusual behavior is due to the existence of two liquid states. Taste and smell
Pure water is often described as tasteless and odorless, although humans have specific sensors that can sense the presence of water in their mouths, and frogs are known to be able to smell it. However, common spring water (including mineral water) often has many dissolved substances that can give it different tastes and smells. Humans and other animals have developed senses that allow them to assess the potability of water to avoid water that is too salty or putrid. Color and appearance
Pure water is visibly blue due to absorption of light in the c region. 600-800 nm. The color can be easily observed in a glass of tap water placed on a pure white background, in daylight. The main absorption bands responsible for the color are the harmonics of the O–H stretching vibrations. The apparent color intensity increases with the depth of the water column, following Beer's law. This also applies, for example, to a swimming pool when the light source is sunlight reflected from the white pool tiles. In nature, the color can also be changed from blue to green due to the presence of suspended solids or algae. In industry, near-infrared spectroscopy is used with aqueous solutions, since the higher intensity of the lower tones in water means that glass cuvettes with a short path length can be used. To observe the fundamental stretching absorption spectrum of water or an aqueous solution in the region around 3500 cm−1 (2.85 μm) a path length of about 25 μm is required. In addition, the cuvette must be transparent around 3500 cm−1 and insoluble in water; Calcium fluoride is a commonly used material for cuvette windows with aqueous solutions. Active Raman fundamental vibrations can be observed, for example, with a 1 cm sample cell. Aquatic plants, algae and other photosynthetic organisms can live in water up to hundreds of meters deep, because sunlight can reach them. Virtually no sunlight reaches the parts of the oceans below 1,000 meters (3,300 feet) deep. The refractive index of liquid water (1.333 at 20 °C (68 °F)) is much higher than that of air (1.0), similar to that of alkanes and ethanol, but lower than that of glycerol (1.473), benzene (1.501), carbon disulfide (1.627), and common types of glass (1.64). The refractive index of ice (1.31) is lower than that of liquid water. Molecular polarity
In a water molecule, the hydrogen atoms form an angle of 104.5° with the oxygen atom. The hydrogen atoms are near two corners of a tetrahedron centered on oxygen. In the other two corners there are lone pairs of valence electrons that do not participate in the bond. In a perfect tetrahedron, the atoms would form an angle of 109.5°, but the repulsion between the lone pairs is greater than the repulsion between the hydrogen atoms. The O–H bond length is approximately 0.096 nm. Other substances have a tetrahedral molecular structure, for example methane (CH4) and hydrogen sulphide (H2S). However, oxygen is more electronegative than most other elements, so the oxygen atom has a partial negative charge while the hydrogen atoms are partially positively charged. Together with the folded structure, this gives the molecule an electric dipole moment and is classified as a polar molecule. Water is a good polar solvent, dissolving many salts and hydrophilic organic molecules such as sugars and simple alcohols such as ethanol. Water also dissolves many gases, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide; the latter gives the effervescence of carbonated drinks, sparkling wines and beers. In addition, many substances in living organisms, such as proteins, DNA and polysaccharides, are dissolved in water. The interactions between water and the subunits of these biomacromolecules shape protein folding, DNA base pairing, and other phenomena crucial to life (hydrophobic effect). Many organic substances (such as fats and oils and alkanes) are hydrophobic, that is, insoluble in water. Many inorganic substances are also insoluble, including most metal oxides, sulfides, and silicates. Hydrogen bond
Due to its polarity, a water molecule in liquid or solid state can form up to four hydrogen bonds with neighboring molecules. Hydrogen bonds are about ten times stronger than the Van der Waals force that attracts molecules to each other in most liquids. This is the reason why the melting and boiling points of water are much higher than those of other analogous compounds such as hydrogen sulfide. They also account for its exceptionally high specific heat capacity (about 4.2 J/(g·K)), heat of fusion (about 333 J/g), heat of vaporization (2257 J/g), and thermal conductivity (between 0.561 and 0.679 W/(m·K)). These properties make water more effective at moderating Earth's climate by storing heat and transporting it between the oceans and the atmosphere. Water's hydrogen bonds are around 23 kJ/mol (compared to an O–H covalent bond at 492 kJ/mol). Of this, it is estimated that 90% is attributable to electrostatics, while the remaining 10% is partially covalent. These bonds are the cause of the high surface tension of water and capillary forces. Capillary action refers to the tendency of water to move through a narrow tube against the force of gravity. All vascular plants, such as trees, rely on this property. Autoionization
Water is a weak solution of hydronium hydroxide – there is an equilibrium 2H2O ⇌ H3O+ + OH–, in combination with the solvation of the resulting hydronium and hydroxide ions. Electrical conductivity and electrolysis
Pure water has a low electrical conductivity, which increases with the dissolution of a small amount of ionic material such as common salt. Liquid water can be split into the elements hydrogen and oxygen by passing an electric current through it, a process called electrolysis. The decomposition requires more energy than the heat released by the reverse process (285.8 kJ/mol, or 15.9 MJ/kg). Mechanical properties
Liquid water can be assumed to be incompressible for most purposes: its compressibility ranges from 4.4 to 5.1×10−10 Pa−1 under ordinary conditions. Even in the oceans at a depth of 4 km, where the pressure is 400 atm, the water undergoes only a 1.8% decrease in volume. The viscosity of water is about 10−3 Pa·s or 0.01 poise at 20 °C (68 °F), and the speed of sound in liquid water varies between 1,400 and 1,540 meters per second (4,600 and 5,100 ft/s) depending on the temperature. Sound travels long distances in water with little attenuation, especially at low frequencies (about 0.03 dB/km for 1 kHz), a property exploited by cetaceans and humans for communication and environmental sensing (sonar). reactivity
Metallic elements that are more electropositive than hydrogen, especially the alkali metals and, to a lesser extent, the alkaline earth metals, displace hydrogen from water, forming hydroxides and releasing hydrogen. At high temperatures, coke, a form of carbon, reacts with steam to form carbon monoxide and hydrogen. On Earth
Hydrology is the study of the movement, distribution and quality of water throughout the Earth. The study of water distribution is hydrography. The study of the distribution and movement of groundwater is hydrogeology, of glaciers is glaciology, of inland waters is limnology, and the distribution of oceans is oceanography. Ecological processes with hydrology are the focus of ecohydrology. The collective mass of water found in, under, and on the surface of a planet is called the hydrosphere. The approximate volume of water on Earth (the world's total water supply) is 1.386 billion cubic kilometers (333 million cubic miles). Liquid water is found in bodies of water, such as an ocean, sea, lake, river, stream, canal, pond, or puddle. Most of the water on Earth is sea water. Water is also present in the atmosphere in solid, liquid and vapor states. It also exists as groundwater in aquifers. Water is important in many geological processes. Groundwater is present in most rocks, and the pressure of this groundwater affects fault patterns. Mantle water is responsible for the melting that produces volcanoes in subduction zones. On Earth's surface, water is important in both chemical and physical weathering processes. Water, and to a lesser but significant extent, ice, are also responsible for a large amount of sediment transport that occurs on the earth's surface. The deposition of transported sediments forms many types of sedimentary rocks, which constitute the geologic record of Earth's history. water cycle
The water cycle (scientifically known as the hydrological cycle) is the continuous exchange of water within the hydrosphere, between the atmosphere, soil water, surface water, groundwater and plants. Water moves perpetually through each of these regions in the water cycle, which consists of the following transfer processes:

the evaporation of oceans and other bodies of water into the air and the transpiration of land plants and animals into the air. precipitation, from water vapor that condenses from the air and falls to land or the ocean. runoff from land that often reaches the sea. Most of the water vapor found primarily in the ocean returns to it, but winds carry water vapor over land at the same rate as runoff to the sea, about 47 Tt per year, while evaporation and transpiration from land masses also contribute another 72 Tt per year. Precipitation, at a rate of 119 Tt per year over land, takes several forms: most commonly rain, snow and hail, with some contribution from fog and dew. Dew is small water droplets that condense when a high density of water vapor meets a cold surface. Dew usually forms in the morning when the temperature is at its lowest, just before sunrise and when the earth's surface temperature begins to rise. Condensed water in the air can also refract sunlight to produce rainbows. Water runoff is often collected in basins that flow into rivers. Through erosion, runoff shapes the environment by creating river valleys and deltas that provide rich soil and level ground for the establishment of population centers. A flood occurs when an area of ​​land, usually low-lying, is covered with water, which occurs when a river overflows its banks or a storm surge occurs. On the other hand, a drought is an extended period of months or years when a region experiences a deficiency in its water supply. This occurs when a region receives consistently below average rainfall, either because of its topography or its location in terms of latitude. Water resources
Water resources are natural water resources that are potentially useful to humans, for example as a source of drinking water supply or irrigation water. Water occurs as "stocks" and "fluids". Water can be stored as lakes, water vapor, groundwater or aquifers, and ice and snow. Of the total volume of global freshwater, an estimated 69 percent is stored in glaciers and permanent snowpack; 30 percent is in groundwater; and the remaining 1 percent in lakes, rivers, atmosphere and biota. The length of time that water remains stored is highly variable: some aquifers consist of water stored for thousands of years, but lake volumes can vary seasonally, decreasing in dry periods and increasing in wet periods. A substantial fraction of the water supply for some regions consists of water withdrawn from water stored in reserves, and when withdrawals exceed recharge, stocks decline. According to some estimates, up to 30 percent of the total water used for irrigation comes from unsustainable groundwater extractions, leading to groundwater depletion. Sea water and tides
Seawater contains an average of 3.5% sodium chloride, in addition to smaller amounts of other substances. The physical properties of seawater differ from fresh water in some important ways. It freezes at a lower temperature (about −1.9 °C (28.6 °F)) and its density increases with decreasing temperature to the freezing point, rather than reaching maximum density at a temperature above freezing. The salinity of water in the main seas varies from about 0.7% in the Baltic Sea to 4.0% in the Red Sea. (The Dead Sea, known for its ultra-high salinity levels of 30-40%, is actually a salt lake.) Tides are the cyclical rise and fall of local sea level caused by the tidal forces of the Moon and Sun acting on the oceans. Tides cause changes in the depth of marine and estuarine water bodies and produce oscillating currents known as tidal currents. The changing tide produced at a given location is the result of changes in the position of the Moon and the Sun in relation to the Earth combined with the effects of the Earth's rotation and local bathymetry. The strip of coastline that is submerged during high tide and exposed during low tide, the intertidal zone, is an important ecological product of ocean tides. Effects on life
From a biological point of view, water has many distinct properties that are critical for the proliferation of life. It performs this function by allowing organic compounds to react in ways that ultimately allow replication. All known life forms depend on water. Water is vital both as a solvent in which many of the body's solutes dissolve and as an essential part of many metabolic processes within the body. Metabolism is the sum total of anabolism and catabolism. In anabolism, water is removed from molecules (via energy requiring enzymatic chemical reactions) to grow larger molecules (eg, starches, triglycerides, and proteins to store fuels and information). In catabolism, water is used to break bonds and generate smaller molecules (eg glucose, fatty acids and amino acids to be used as fuels for energy use or other purposes). Without water, these particular metabolic processes could not exist. Water is essential for both photosynthesis and respiration. Photosynthetic cells use energy from the sun to separate hydrogen from water from oxygen. In the presence of sunlight, hydrogen combines with CO2 (absorbed from air or water) to form glucose and release oxygen. All living cells use such fuels and oxidize hydrogen and carbon to capture energy from the sun and reform water and CO2 in the process (cellular respiration). Water is also critical for acid-base neutrality and enzyme function. An acid, a hydrogen ion donor (H+, that is, a proton), can be neutralized by a base, a proton acceptor such as a hydroxide ion (OH−) to form water. Water is considered neutral, with a pH (the negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration) of 7 in an ideal state. Acids have pH values below 7 while bases have values above 7. Aquatic life forms
Earth's surface waters are teeming with life. The first forms of life appeared in water; almost all fish live exclusively in water, and there are many types of marine mammals, such as dolphins and whales. Some types of animals, such as amphibians, spend part of their lives in water and part of their lives on land. Plants such as algae and algae grow in water and are the basis of some underwater ecosystems. Plankton is generally the base of the ocean food chain. Aquatic vertebrates must obtain oxygen to survive, and they do so in a number of ways. Fish have gills instead of lungs, although some species of fish, such as the lungfish, have both. Marine mammals such as dolphins, whales, otters and seals need to come to the surface periodically to breathe air. Some amphibians are able to absorb oxygen through their skin. Invertebrates exhibit a wide range of modifications to survive in poorly oxygenated waters, including breathing tubes (see insect and mollusk siphons) and gills (Carcinus). However, because invertebrate life evolved in an aquatic habitat, most have little or no specialization for respiration in water. Effects on human civilization
Civilization has historically flourished around rivers and major waterways; Mesopotamia, one of the so-called cradles of civilization, was located between the main rivers Tigris and Euphrates; the ancient society of the Egyptians was entirely dependent on the Nile. The first Indus Valley Civilization (c. 3300 BC - c. 1300 BC) developed along the Indus River and its tributaries flowing out of the Himalayas. Rome was also founded on the banks of the Italian Tiber River. Large metropolises such as Rotterdam, London, Montreal, Paris, New York, Buenos Aires, Shanghai, Tokyo, Chicago and Hong Kong owe their success in part to their easy accessibility by water and the consequent expansion of trade. Islands with safe water harbors, such as Singapore, flourished for the same reason. In places like North Africa and the Middle East, where water is more scarce, access to clean water has been and is an important factor in human development. Health and pollution
Water suitable for human consumption is called potable or potable water. Water that is not potable can be made potable by filtration or distillation, or by other methods. More than 660 million people do not have access to clean water. Water that is not suitable for drinking but is not harmful to humans when used for swimming or bathing is called by various names other than potable or potable water, and is sometimes called potable or "safe for bathing". Chlorine is a skin and mucous membrane irritant that is used to make water safe for bathing or drinking. Its use is highly technical and often controlled by government regulations (typically 1 part per million (ppm) for drinking water and 1-2 ppm of unreacted chlorine with impurities for bathing water). Bath water can be maintained in satisfactory microbiological conditions by chemical disinfectants such as chlorine or ozone or by the use of ultraviolet light. Water reclamation is the process of converting wastewater (more commonly sewage, also called municipal wastewater) into water that can be reused for other purposes. There are 2.3 billion people living in water-scarce countries, meaning that each individual receives less than 1,700 cubic meters (60,000 cubic feet) of water per year. 380 billion cubic meters (13×10^12 cubic feet) of municipal wastewater is produced each year. Freshwater is a renewable resource, recirculated by the natural hydrological cycle, but pressures on access to it result from its naturally uneven distribution in space and time, the growing economic demands of agriculture and industry, and population growth. Currently, almost one billion people around the world do not have access to safe and affordable water. In 2000, the United Nations set the Millennium Development Goals for water to halve by 2015 the proportion of people worldwide without access to clean water and sanitation. Progress towards that goal has been uneven, and in 2015 the UN committed to the Sustainable Development Goals to achieve universal access to safe and affordable water and sanitation by 2030. Poor water quality and poor sanitation are deadly; about five million deaths a year are caused by water-related diseases. The World Health Organization estimates that clean water could prevent 1.4 million child deaths from diarrhea each year. In developing countries, 90% of all municipal wastewater remains untreated in local rivers and streams. About 50 countries, with roughly a third of the world's population, also suffer from medium or high water scarcity and 17 of these annually extract more water than is recharged through their natural cycles. The strain not only affects surface freshwater bodies such as rivers and lakes, but also degrades groundwater resources. Human uses
Agriculture
The most important human use of water is for agriculture, including irrigated agriculture, which accounts for 80 to 90 percent of total human water use. In the United States, 42% of fresh water withdrawn for use is for irrigation, but the vast majority of water "consumed" (used and not returned to the environment) goes to agriculture. Access to fresh water is often taken for granted, especially in developed countries that have built sophisticated water systems to collect, purify and deliver water and dispose of wastewater. But growing economic, demographic and climate pressures are raising concerns about water issues, leading to increasing competition for fixed water resources, giving rise to the concept of peak water. As populations and economies continue to grow, water-hungry meat consumption expands, and new demands for biofuels or new water-intensive industries rise, new water challenges are likely. In 2007, the International Water Management Institute in Sri Lanka conducted an assessment of water management in agriculture to see if the world had enough water to provide food for its growing population. It assessed the current availability of water for agriculture on a global scale and outlined the places suffering from water shortages. It found that a fifth of the world's population, more than 1.2 billion, live in areas of physical water scarcity, where there is not enough water to meet all demands. Another 1.6 billion people live in areas experiencing economic water scarcity, where a lack of investment in water or insufficient human capacity makes it impossible for authorities to meet water demand. The report found that it would be possible to produce the necessary food in the future, but that continued food production and current environmental trends would lead to crises in many parts of the world. To avoid a global water crisis, farmers will need to strive to increase productivity to meet growing food demands, while industries and cities find ways to use water more efficiently. Water scarcity is also caused by the production of water-intensive products. For example, cotton: 1 kg of cotton (equivalent to a pair of jeans) requires 10.9 cubic meters (380 cubic feet) of water to produce. Although cotton accounts for 2.4% of global water use, the water is consumed in regions that are already at risk of water scarcity. Significant environmental damage has been caused: for example, the former Soviet Union's diversion of water from the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to produce cotton was largely responsible for the disappearance of the Aral Sea. As a scientific standard
On April 7, 1795, the gram was defined in France as "the absolute weight of a volume of pure water equal to a cube of one-hundredth of a meter, and at the melting temperature of ice." However, for practical purposes, a metallic reference standard, a thousand times more massive, the kilogram, was required. That is why a work was commissioned to accurately determine the mass of a liter of water. Although the decreed definition of the gram specified water at 0 °C (32 °F)—a highly reproducible temperature—scientists chose to redefine the standard and perform their measurements at the temperature of the highest density of water, which was measured at the time as 4 °C (39 °F). The Kelvin temperature scale of the SI system was based on the triple point of water, defined as exactly 273.16 K (0.01 °C; 32.02 °F), but as of May 2019 is based on Boltzmann's constant. The scale is an absolute temperature scale with the same increments as the Celsius temperature scale, which was originally defined by the boiling point (set at 100 °C (212 °F)) and melting point (set at 0 °C (32 °F)) of water. Natural water consists mainly of the isotopes hydrogen-1 and oxygen-16, but there is also a small amount of the heavier isotopes oxygen-18, oxygen-17 and hydrogen-2 (deuterium). The percentage of heavier isotopes is very small, but it still affects the properties of water. Water from rivers and lakes usually contains less heavy isotopes than seawater. Standard water is therefore defined in the Vienna standard ocean mean water specification. to drink
The human body contains, on average, 50-60% water, depending on age, sex and body size, although individuals can have between 45% and 75%. The US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine recommend a daily intake of 3.7 liters of water for adult men and 2.7 liters for women. The exact amount depends on activity level, temperature, humidity and other factors. Most of this is ingested through food or beverages other than drinking water. Medical literature favors lower consumption, typically 1 liter of water for the average male, excluding additional requirements due to fluid loss from exercise or hot weather. Healthy kidneys can excrete 0.8 to 1 liter of water per hour, but stress such as exercise can reduce this amount. People can drink far more water than necessary while exercising, which can put them at risk of water intoxication (hyperhydration), which can be fatal. The popular claim that "a person should consume eight glasses of water a day" seems to have no real basis in science. Chemical uses
Water is widely used in chemical reactions as a solvent or reactant and less commonly as a solute or catalyst. In inorganic reactions, water is a common solvent, dissolving many ionic compounds as well as other polar compounds such as ammonia and compounds closely related to water. In organic reactions, it is not usually used as a reaction solvent, because it does not dissolve reagents well and is amphoteric (acidic and basic) and nucleophilic. However, these properties are sometimes desirable. Acceleration of Diels-Alder reactions by water has also been observed. Supercritical water has recently been a research topic. Supercritical water saturated with oxygen burns organic pollutants efficiently. Heat exchange
Water and steam are a common fluid used for heat exchange, due to their availability and high heat capacity, both for cooling and for heating. Cold water may even be available naturally in a lake or sea. It is particularly effective at transporting heat through the vaporization and condensation of water due to its large latent heat of vaporization. One disadvantage is that metals commonly found in industries such as steel and copper are oxidized faster by untreated water and steam. In almost all thermal power plants, water is used as the working fluid (used in a closed loop between the boiler, steam turbine and condenser) and the coolant (used to exchange waste heat to a body of water or take it by evaporation in a cooling tower). In the United States, cooling power plants are the largest user of water. In the nuclear industry, water can also be used as a neutron moderator. In most nuclear reactors, water is both a coolant and a moderator. This provides a sort of passive safety measure, as removing the water from the reactor also slows down the nuclear reaction. However, other methods of stopping a reaction are preferred and it is preferred to keep the nuclear core covered with water to ensure adequate cooling. Considerations about fire
Water has a high heat of vaporization and is relatively inert, making it a good fire extinguishing fluid. Evaporation of water takes the heat away from the fire. It is dangerous to use water on fires involving oils and organic solvents because many organic materials float on water and the water tends to spread the burning liquid. The use of water in firefighting must also take into account the dangers of a steam explosion, which can occur when water is used on very hot fires in confined spaces, and a hydrogen explosion, when substances that react with water, such as certain metals or hot carbon such as coal, charcoal or graphite coke, break down the water, producing gaseous water. The power of such explosions was seen in the Chernobyl disaster, although the water involved in this case did not come from firefighting but from the reactor's own water cooling system. A steam explosion occurred when the extreme overheating of the core caused the water to turn into steam. A hydrogen explosion may have occurred as a result of a reaction between the steam and the hot zirconium. Some metal oxides, especially those of the alkali metals and alkaline earth metals, produce so much heat in reaction with water that a fire hazard can develop. Alkaline earth oxide quicklime, also known as calcium oxide, is a mass-produced substance that is often transported in paper bags. If they get soaked, they can ignite as their contents react with water. recreation

Water industry
The water industry provides drinking water and wastewater services (including sewage treatment) to households and industry. Water supply facilities include water wells, rainwater collection cisterns, water supply networks and water purification facilities, water tanks, water towers, water pipelines, including old aqueducts. Atmospheric water generators are under development. Drinking water is usually collected from springs, drawn from artificial wells (wells) in the ground, or pumped from lakes and rivers. Building more wells in suitable locations is therefore one possible way to produce more water, assuming the aquifers can provide adequate flow. Other sources of water include rainwater harvesting. Water may require purification for human consumption. This can involve the removal of undissolved substances, dissolved substances and harmful microbes. Popular methods are sand filtration which only removes undissolved material, while chlorination and boiling kill harmful microbes. Distillation does all three functions. There are more advanced techniques, such as reverse osmosis. Desalination of abundant seawater is a more expensive solution used in arid coastal climates. Drinking water is distributed through municipal water systems, delivered by tanker truck or as bottled water. Governments in many countries have programs to distribute water to those in need at no cost. Reducing use by using potable water only for human consumption is another option. In some cities such as Hong Kong, seawater is widely used to flush toilets throughout the city to conserve fresh water resources. Polluting water may be the greatest misuse of water; to the extent that a pollutant limits other uses of water, it becomes a waste of the resource, regardless of the benefits to the polluter. Like other types of pollution, this does not enter the standard accounting of market costs, conceived as externalities that the market cannot account for. Thus, other people pay the price of water pollution, while the profits of private companies are not redistributed to the local population, victims of this pollution. Pharmaceuticals consumed by humans often end up in waterways and can have harmful effects on aquatic life if they bioaccumulate and are not biodegradable. Municipal and industrial waste water is normally treated in sewage treatment plants. Mitigation of contaminated surface runoff is addressed through a variety of prevention and treatment techniques. Many industrial processes rely on reactions using chemicals dissolved in water, suspending solids in water slurries, or using water to dissolve and extract substances, or to wash products or process equipment. Processes such as mining, chemical pulping, pulp bleaching, papermaking, textile production, dyeing, printing and power plant cooling use large amounts of water, requiring a dedicated water source and often causing significant water pollution. Water is used in power generation. Hydroelectricity is electricity obtained from hydroelectric power. Hydroelectric power comes from water that drives a water turbine connected to a generator. Hydroelectricity is a low-cost, non-polluting source of renewable energy. Energy is supplied by the movement of water. Usually a dam is built on a river, creating an artificial lake behind it. The water that comes out of the lake is forced through turbines that make the generators. Pressurized water is used in water jets and water jet cutters. High pressure water guns are used for precise cutting. It works great, it's relatively safe, and it's not harmful to the environment. Water is also used in many industrial processes and machines, such as the steam turbine and heat exchanger, in addition to its use as a chemical solvent. The discharge of untreated water from industrial uses is pollution. Pollution includes discharged solutes (chemical pollution) and discharged cooling water (thermal pollution). Industry requires pure water for many applications and uses a variety of purification techniques in both water supply and disposal. The digital sector, especially artificial intelligence, uses a lot of water, so the expansion of AI can "threaten global and national water security." Food processing
Boiling, steaming, and simmering are popular cooking methods that often require immersing food in water or its gaseous state, steam. Water is also used for washing dishes. Water also plays many critical roles in the field of food science. Solutes such as salts and sugars found in water affect the physical properties of water. The boiling and freezing points of water are affected by solutes, as well as air pressure, which in turn is affected by altitude. Water boils at lower temperatures with the lower air pressure that occurs at higher temperatures. One mole of sucrose (sugar) per kilogram of water raises the boiling point of water by 0.51 °C (0.918 °F), and one mole of salt per kg raises the boiling point by 1.02 °C (1.836 °F); similarly, increasing the number of dissolved particles lowers the freezing point of water. Solutes in water also affect water activity which affects many chemical reactions and microbial growth in food. Water activity can be described as the ratio of the vapor pressure of water in a solution to the vapor pressure of pure water. Solutes in water reduce water activity: This is important to know because most bacterial growth ceases at low levels of water activity. Not only does microbial growth affect food safety, but also food preservation and shelf life. Water hardness is also a critical factor in food processing and can be altered or treated using a chemical ion exchange system. It can dramatically affect the quality of a product, as well as play a role in sanitation. Water hardness is classified based on the concentration of calcium carbonate that the water contains. Water is classified as soft if it contains less than 100 mg/L (UK) or less than 60 mg/L (US). According to a report published by the organization Water Footprint in 2010, a single kilogram of meat requires 15 thousand liters (3.3×10^3 imp gal; 4.0×10^3 US gal) of water; however, the authors also make it clear that this is a global average and that circumstantial factors determine the amount of water used in beef production. In the universe
Much of the water in the universe is produced as a byproduct of star formation. Star formation is accompanied by a strong outer wind of gas and dust. When this outflow of material finally impacts the surrounding gas, the shock waves that are created compress and heat the gas. The observed water is produced rapidly in this hot dense gas. On July 22, 2011, a report described the discovery of a giant cloud of water vapor containing "140 trillion times more water than all of Earth's oceans combined" around a quasar located 12 billion light-years from Earth. According to the researchers, the "discovery shows that water has prevailed in the universe for almost its entire existence." Water has been detected in the interstellar clouds of the Milky Way. Water probably also exists in abundance in other galaxies, because its components, hydrogen and oxygen, are among the most abundant elements in the universe. Water and planetary habitability
The existence of liquid water, and to a lesser extent its gaseous and solid forms, on Earth are vital to the existence of life on Earth as we know it. The Earth is located in the habitable zone of the Solar System; if it were a little closer or further away from the Sun (about 5%, or about 8 million kilometers), the conditions that allow all three forms to exist simultaneously would be much less likely to exist. The size of the Earth also plays a role: its gravity allows it to maintain an atmosphere, including air moisture. Smaller planets like Mars have extremely thin or no atmospheres. Water vapor and other greenhouse gases in the atmosphere provide a temperature buffer (greenhouse effect) that helps maintain a relatively stable surface temperature when present in regular amounts. The state of water on a planet depends on the environmental pressure, which is determined by the planet's gravity. If a planet is massive enough, the water on it can be solid even at high temperatures, due to the high pressure caused by gravity, as observed in the exoplanets Gliese 436 b and GJ 1214 b. Law, politics and crisis
Water policy is the policy affected by water and water resources. Water, especially fresh water, is a strategic resource around the world and an important element in many political conflicts. It causes impacts on health and damage to biodiversity. Access to clean water has improved over the past few decades in almost all parts of the world, but nearly one billion people still do not have access to clean water and more than 2.5 billion lack access to adequate sanitation. However, some observers have estimated that by 2025 more than half of the world's population will face water-based vulnerability. A report, published in November 2009, suggests that by 2030, in some developing regions of the world, demand for water will exceed supply by 50%. 1.6 billion people have had access to a source of clean water since 1990. The proportion of people in developing countries with access to clean water is estimated to have improved from 30% in 1970 to 71% in 1990, 79% in 2000 and 84% in 2004. A 2006 report states that access to water is sufficient for everyone. hampered by mismanagement and corruption. In addition, global initiatives to improve the efficiency of aid delivery, such as the Paris Declaration on Aid Effectiveness, have not been adopted by donors in the water sector as effectively as in education and health, which can leave multiple donors working on overlapping projects and recipient governments unable to act. The authors of the 2007 Comprehensive Assessment of Water Management in Agriculture cited poor governance as one of the reasons for some forms of water scarcity. Water governance is the set of formal and informal processes through which decisions related to water management are made. Good water governance is primarily about knowing which processes work best in a particular physical and socio-economic context. Mistakes have sometimes been made in trying to apply "blueprints" that work in the developed world to places and contexts in the developing world. The Mekong River is an example; an International Water Management Institute review of the policies of six countries that depend on the Mekong River for water found that thorough and transparent cost-benefit analyzes and environmental impact assessments were rarely conducted. They also found that Cambodia's water bill was far more complex than it needed to be. In 2004, the UK charity WaterAid reported that a child dies every 15 seconds from easily preventable water-related diseases, which are often linked to a lack of adequate sanitation. Since 2003, the UN World Water Development Report, produced by UNESCO's World Water Assessment Programme, has provided decision-makers with tools to develop sustainable water policies. The 2023 report notes that two billion people (26% of the population) do not have access to safe drinking water and 3.6 billion (46%) lack access to safely managed sanitation. People in urban areas (2.4 billion) will face water scarcity by 2050. Water scarcity has been described as endemic, due to overconsumption and pollution. The report notes that 10% of the world's population lives in countries with high or critical water stress. However, over the past 40 years, water consumption has increased by about 1% per year, and is expected to grow at the same rate until 2050. Since 2000, flooding in the tropics has increased fourfold, while flooding in the northern mid-latitudes has increased 2.5 times. The cost of these floods between 2000 and 2019 was 100,000 deaths and 650 million dollars. Organizations concerned with water protection include the International Water Association (IWA), WaterAid, Water 1st and the American Water Resources Association. The International Water Management Institute carries out projects with the aim of using effective water management to reduce poverty. Conventions related to water are the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD), the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships, the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea and the Ramsar Convention. religion
Water is considered a purifier in most religions. Faiths that incorporate ritual washing (ablution) include Christianity, Hinduism, Islam, Judaism, the Rastafarian movement, Shinto, Taoism, and Wicca. The immersion (or aspersion or fusion) of a person in water is a central sacrament of Christianity (where it is called baptism); it is also part of the practice of other religions, including Islam (Ghusl), Judaism (mikvah), and Sikhism (Amrit Sanskar). Also, in many religions, including Islam and Judaism, a ritual bath in pure water is performed for the dead. In Islam, the five daily prayers can be performed in most cases after washing certain parts of the body with clean water (wudu), unless water is not available (see Tayammum). In Shinto, water is used in almost all rituals to cleanse a person or an area (for example, in the misogi ritual). In Christianity, holy water is water that has been sanctified by a priest for the purpose of baptism, the blessing of people, places and objects, or as a means of repelling evil. In Zoroastrianism, water (āb) is respected as the source of life. philosophy
The Greek philosopher Empedocles saw water as one of the four classical elements (along with fire, earth and air) and regarded it as an ilema or basic substance of the universe. Thales, whom Aristotle described as an astronomer and an engineer, theorized that the earth, which is denser than water, arose from water. Thales, a monist, further believed that all things are made of water. Plato believed that the shape of water is an icosahedron, which flows easily compared to the cube-shaped earth. The theory of the four bodily humors associated water with phlegm, as cold and moist. The classical element of water was also one of the five elements of traditional Chinese philosophy (along with earth, fire, wood and metal). Some traditional and popular Asian philosophical systems take water as a model. James Legge's 1891 translation of the Dao De Jing states: "The highest excellence is like (that of) water. The excellence of water appears for the benefit of all things, and in its occupation, without striving (on the contrary), the low place that all men dislike.
